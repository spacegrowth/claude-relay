🚦 relay

Several Claude sessions.
One of them is in charge.

A Claude Code plugin. The lead session plans the work and hands it to executor sessions in their own terminal tabs. It reads every diff. You decide every commit.

Delegate. Review. Commit.

Point at the headline and let the picture land: this is a row of terminal tabs, not agents in a box. The three verbs at the bottom are the whole talk. Come back to them in the demo.

Concede the overlap immediately: teams also get their own context, their own panes, and direct messaging. The difference is that relay treats the executor as untrusted: it cannot commit, its report is machine-checked against the index, and you approve the plan. Facts for Q&A: agent teams need CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_AGENT_TEAMS=1; teammate model falls through to the lead's; effort is always the lead's; permission modes can't be set per teammate at spawn; a named subagent becomes a teammate without confirmation; one team per session; the lead can't be handed off. Relay handles the last one with /relay:handoff.

how it works

The best model makes the decisions. Cheaper ones do the typing.

Judgment is what's expensive to get wrong: what to build, whether a diff is really mergeable, when a report is lying to you. That's the lead. Everything else is a packet.

[Lead] your projectplans, splits into packets
writes one
reads diff + report
commits
1 · packet file: one bounded piece of the plan
3 · report file + staged changes
🚦 4 · relay wakes the lead: review needed
[Exec] topic2 · works in the shared worktree
stages, never commits
writes the report

The wake comes from your side, not the executor's: a hook in the lead's session watches for the report, then announces 🚦 [relay] — review needed.

Executors can't commit (denied at launch). Leads can't make large inline edits (a hook blocks it). The asymmetry is the design.

The headline is the thesis; the diagram is the proof. Start with the plan, not the arrows: splitting the work into bounded pieces is the lead's judgment and relay never does it for you. That's the callback on the six-steps slide. Then walk the arrows in order. Stress that the wake originates in the lead's session, from a hook: there is no arrow from the executor to the lead except the report file.

First sentence is the outcome. Then a block relay reads by machine: status, risk flags, what's unverified, which files. The format is in the executor's system prompt, not something you ask for.

clean-with-caveats is a stop on purpose. The other statuses are clean, partial, blocked. The caveats are the point.

Then you read the diff and have the claims checked. Are the named files actually staged, do the counts agree. The best verdict is COUNTS-MATCH, deliberately not "PASS". Then commit, or send a fix-list back into the same executor.

choosing a model

Who gets the packet?

A script could check it's done.

Haiku. Rename sweeps, boilerplate from a shown template, config plumbing. The packet must be tight. If it needs a "why" explained, it isn't Haiku work.

A plausible wrong answer could slip past you.

Opus. Unknown root causes, cross-cutting changes, core logic. Pay for judgment where a review wouldn't catch a wrong answer.

Everything else.

Sonnet. Bounded features, bugs with a repro, refactors inside one module. Most packets land here.

Picked too weak? Third packet into the same session, relay puts a banner on your screen. Say "upgrade" and it moves one tier up. Too strong? The lead's terminal says "haiku can do this" at spawn.

Pass the tier name (haiku / sonnet / opus); relay resolves it through this machine's CLI. Effort: leave it alone. Claude Code already runs at xhigh, and a longer-thinking Sonnet is still a Sonnet — the tier is the lever, not the dial.

Check the cheap case first, then the expensive one; Sonnet is what is left. The "two rounds" rule is the only feedback most people will actually use.

caching, in simple terms

The model forgets everything between turns. The cache is what makes that affordable.

No memory. Every reply, the whole conversation so far is sent to the API again, and billed again.

The cache remembers the start. Anything the API has already seen costs a tenth to send again. Anything new costs 2× to store, once.

It stays warm while you keep talking. Every turn resets a 60-minute clock (signed in with a Claude plan; 5 minutes on an API key unless promptCacheTtl is set). Go quiet for an hour and the next turn re-stores the whole history at 2× — one cold turn costs about twenty warm ones.

what's in the cache: everything sent so far, in this order

system prompt
tool list
the packet
every file it read
every command's output
its own replies
this turn

A file read on turn 3 is re-sent on every turn after. It only matches from the start, and it's per model.

Which is why relay's first answer to a new packet is send into a warm executor, not spawn.

Beat one is the whole idea: no memory, so everything is re-sent. Point at "every file it read" on the strip. The two catches at the bottom are why relay never swaps a session's model and why the system prompt is frozen.

the bill

Why a warm executor is cheap, and a huge one isn't

Warm reuse. The next packet into a session you already have pays 10% on everything it has read. A fresh spawn stores all of it again.

Size is a tax. 10% of 260k is 26k tokens, every single turn, just to remember. A rotation costs one cold start, roughly 200k tokens' worth. Past 150k that pays for itself inside a single packet, so that's where relay draws the line.

Tier is multiples, not percents. Haiku work costs Haiku. Nothing else on this slide moves the bill as much.

reuse, rotate, hand off

Keep using a session while it's cheap. Replace it when it isn't.

Executors. The next packet goes into the one you already have. It's warm and it knows the code. A fresh one is the exception, not the default.

Its context grows with every turn. Past 150k the lead stops sending into it: it retires the session and starts a successor that reads a seed of what the old one did. You lose the conversation, you keep the knowledge.

executor live context, against the 150k line

264k: rotate

The lead. It grows too, more slowly, because it reads reports and diffs instead of code. Same line, 150k. Past it relay nudges once: hand off. The lead writes a note of where things stand, and a fresh lead picks up from the note. Every executor keeps running.

Transcript size is the second signal: 5 MB on disk means several compactions, so the plan in its head is a summary of summaries.

the lead that wrote this deck: live context against the 150k line

321k: hand off  ·  its transcript said 4.5 MB, "getting close"

The first session the 150k gate ever caught was the executor that built it. Why reuse is cheap and size is a tax: caching, a few slides on.

Same idea at two speeds. Executors churn in an afternoon; a lead lasts days. Rotate = retire plus a seeded successor, one step, the lead does it. Handoff = a note plus a fresh lead, and it's the one thing agent teams can't do at all. The 321k / 4.5 MB pair is real: the lead building this deck. MB said "getting close"; tokens said "twice past the line". That's why the nudge went token-first in 0.3.49.

lifecycle

When executors close, and how

Landed

You committed (or discarded) its work, and the files it claimed are clean in the tree. Two minutes later relay closes the tab on its own.

Idle

It reported, you've seen the report, and 60 minutes passed. Closed.

By hand

/relay:close now. /relay:retire to close and write the successor seed. --keep at spawn, or relay keep, pins one you know you'll need.

Closed isn't gone. /relay:send to a closed session reopens the same conversation with everything it knew. /relay:resume does that for a tab that crashed. /relay:restart re-runs the packet in a fresh conversation when you'd rather it forgot.

Nothing is auto-closed until the lead has actually seen its report. Busy, stalled, queued and pinned sessions are never touched.

The question behind this slide is "will I lose work?" No: staged changes live in the worktree, reports live on disk, and the conversation is resumable.

guardrails

Three things relay refuses to let happen

The lead quietly does the work itself.

A large inline edit from a lead is blocked by a hook and told to delegate. Packet files are exempt. /relay:route opens a two-minute window when it really is lead work, and the reason goes in the ledger.

Things happen while you're away.

When the wake fires, the lead announces what's ready and stops. /relay:auto on lets it proceed on its own, but only inside a plan you already approved, and it resets each time lead mode is re-armed.

A bad diff gets committed.

Even in auto, a commit needs all five: verify says COUNTS-MATCH; the report says clean, no risk flags, nothing unverified; the packet was in the plan; nothing sign-off-gated is touched; the lead has actually read the diff.

The last two are attestations the tool can't check. It refuses without them, and a false one is a false statement in the ledger. While building this deck, every commit stopped at the gate because the packets touched lib/lead_guard.py. That's the gate working.

Frame it as the three fears: it'll do the work itself, it'll act while I'm out, it'll commit junk. Each column is the answer to one fear. Don't read the five conditions aloud; say "five, and two of them are the lead's own word".

using it

Six steps

/relay:modeArm the lead. Model check, edit gate on, wake on.
write a packetGoal on the first line, files, acceptance commands, boundaries.
/relay:spawnWorktree, topic, packet, --model sonnet. Or /relay:send into one you have.
waitThe wake announces the report. /relay:check if you're impatient.
/relay:diff /relay:verifyRead the diff. Check the claims.
git commitYou, not the executor. Then relay parks it.

The first spawn of a session always asks for your go, even in auto. Deciding how work splits is yours.
